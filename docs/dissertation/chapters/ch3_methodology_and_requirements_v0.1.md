# Chapter 3 — Research Methodology and Requirements

## 3.1 Methodological approach

The project combined software construction with empirical evaluation, and a design-science orientation was therefore adopted: an artefact was built to address an identified problem, and knowledge claims were derived from the artefact's measured behaviour rather than from its description alone [REFERENCE VERIFICATION REQUIRED: canonical design-science methodology source, e.g. Hevner et al. or Peffers et al., to be cited once verified]. Three complementary strands of evidence were planned: a comparative evaluation of candidate pre-trained models (Experiment E1), a fine-tuning study with before-and-after measurement (Experiment E2), and a system-level validation of the end-to-end workflow against a seeded-defect target application (Experiment E3). The detailed experimental designs are given in Chapter 6.

Development proceeded iteratively, and the iterations were preserved in version control: an initial single-service implementation (16 July 2026) was followed by second- and third-version requirement updates (17 July), the model-evaluation framework (25 July), a user-interface scanning extension on its own branch (5 August), a monorepo restructuring (6 August), completion of model fine-tuning (8 August), and the API-testing, regression-comparison and CI-gating work merged on 15 August. This history was treated as methodological evidence in its own right, since it documents when each capability existed and in what order design decisions were taken.

A fixed reproducibility discipline was applied to the research components: dataset construction, example selection and training were all seeded (seed 20260808), the fine-tuning corpus was generated deterministically from feature specifications, and evaluation prompts were rendered through the production agent code so that the evaluated distribution matched the deployed distribution.

## 3.2 Requirements

The platform was built against a versioned Software Requirements Specification, of which the third version was authoritative during the final development period. The specification documents themselves were not committed to the repository, and their incorporation into this dissertation is pending [EVIDENCE REQUIRED: AI_QA_Agent_Project_Requirements.docx, _V2.docx and _V3.docx]. Their existence and structure are nevertheless independently evidenced in the codebase: requirement identifiers from the specification's functional families are cited throughout the source as tracebility comments, including document ingestion and parsing (FR-ENG and ingestion requirements), automation-framework conventions (FR-AUT-003 to FR-AUT-007, covering accessibility-first locators, prohibition of fixed waits, configuration through fixtures, traceability comments and safe file writing), execution and live-event requirements (FR-EXE-002, FR-EXE-006 to 008), human-in-the-loop gates (FR-HITL-001 and 002), security requirements (SEC-002 to SEC-004, covering secret handling, domain allow-listing and prompt-injection defence), and third-version execution and retention controls (FR-V3-EXE-011/012; FR-V3-ENT-011).

From these traces, the requirements baseline was summarised as follows. Functionally, the system was required to ingest multi-format requirement documents with per-segment provenance; to generate analyses, ten-section test plans, structured test cases and automation code; to gate generation behind validation and versioned human approval; to execute suites in real browsers with step-level telemetry; to classify failures with human override; to produce exportable reports separating measured fact from AI narrative; to compare runs for regressions against a promoted baseline; and to integrate with GitHub for commit, push and workflow dispatch. Non-functionally, the dominant requirements were safety (no untrusted code execution; no privileged self-registration; least-privilege tokens), auditability (append-only audit trail; correlation identifiers end to end), responsiveness of long-running work (asynchronous jobs acknowledged within two seconds; replayable progress), and operation without any cloud dependency (local inference by default, with cloud providers as a per-project option). A full requirements table is provided in Appendix H [EVIDENCE REQUIRED: pending the specification documents].

## 3.3 Evaluation methodology

Measurement was designed around the principle stated in RQ2: that AI-generated artefacts must be judged by measurable criteria rather than impression. A rubric was defined with per-task weights across accuracy, completeness, requirement coverage, hallucination, consistency, executability, code quality, speed, reliability and satisfaction, together with a headline research score; deterministic metrics were computed by reusing the platform's own validation gates, coverage reporting and duplicate detection, while semantic consistency used embedding similarity. An LLM-as-judge component and a satisfaction instrument (the System Usability Scale) were designed but were not executed, and no result derived from them is reported; the consequences of this reduction are analysed as a threat to validity in Chapter 8. For the fine-tuning study, evaluation was performed on a held-out application family that the models had never seen in training, using the production agents end to end, so that improvement claims reflected the deployed pipeline rather than isolated prompt performance.

A further methodological rule was adopted for this dissertation after an audit of the collected evidence: only results traceable to raw, machine-readable artefacts (databases, logs, JUnit files, training telemetry) are reported. Summary figures whose provenance could not be established were excluded rather than caveated.

## 3.4 Ethical considerations and data handling

The project was designed to require no human participants, and none were used; accordingly no participant information sheet or consent procedure applies. Ethical clearance was addressed in the approved project proposal (module CSC-40040, 17 June 2026), which committed the project to open-source, demonstration or synthetic target applications and to the exclusion of private customer data, production logs and personally identifiable information. The formal ethical review outcome is to be included at Appendix A [EVIDENCE REQUIRED: ethical approval form or confirmation of the module's ethics process].

Data handling followed that commitment. All evaluation inputs were synthetic or self-authored: the demonstration application and its user stories were written for the project; the fine-tuning corpus consisted of twenty-seven feature specifications for eight fictional applications, with labels rendered deterministically from the same specifications, so no third-party dataset, licence or scraped content was involved. Secrets were handled by reference rather than value — test data in the corpus named environment variables instead of embedding credentials — and the platform masked secret-like values in logs, telemetry and reports. Employer-related material that had been used incidentally during development was identified in the audit and excluded from the dissertation and its appendices; the repository copies were scheduled for anonymisation before any submission of source code [EVIDENCE REQUIRED: confirmation of the anonymisation decisions in register §10]. One external website (a public test-practice site) appeared in a single training example; it was used read-only and in accordance with its purpose as a practice target.

## 3.5 Tool selection rationale

Tooling decisions were made against the requirements above rather than by novelty. Playwright with pytest was selected for execution because of first-class trace, screenshot and video evidence, web-first assertions that discourage timing-based flakiness, and marker-based suite selection that could be made deterministic. Ollama was selected as the default inference host because local models satisfied the no-cloud-dependency requirement and kept requirement documents on-premises; the Qwen2.5 family (7B, instruct and coder variants) was chosen as the strongest locally runnable candidates at the time, with an OpenAI-compatible client providing optional cloud access for comparison. QLoRA on Apple-silicon MLX was chosen for fine-tuning because it permitted adaptation of 7B models within 16 GB of unified memory. NestJS with TypeORM and React with TanStack Query were selected for the backend and frontend on the grounds of typed contracts and mature real-time support; these choices are evaluated critically in Chapter 8.
